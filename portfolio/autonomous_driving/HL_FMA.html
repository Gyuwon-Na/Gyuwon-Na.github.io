1/5-Scale Vehicle Platform

AUG 2025 - SEP 2025 Systems Engineer Encouragement Award 2025 HL Future Mobility Award
ROS1 Noetic Path Following Autonomous Parking GPS-IMU Fusion

Overview

This project was a ROS1-based autonomous driving system for a 1/5-scale vehicle, developed for the 2025 HL Future Mobility Award. The vehicle had to follow a predefined course while executing a sequence of forward driving, T-shaped reversing, parallel parking, traffic-light stopping, and a timed stop on a slope. Because the mission combined multiple driving directions and tightly spaced parking paths, reliable state transitions were as important as basic path tracking.

As a systems engineer, I worked on localization input, mission sequencing, path-following behavior, and vehicle-level tuning. I connected the GPS and IMU fusion output to the driving stack, organized the course into direction-specific path segments, and refined endpoint, speed, and slope-handling logic through repeated field tests. The team received an Encouragement Award at the competition.

Key Achievements

  • Competition Recognition: Received an Encouragement Award at the 2025 HL Future Mobility Award.
  • Stable Heading Input: Integrated a Kalman-filtered GPS and IMU heading estimate through the /imu_gps_heading ROS topic for path tracking.
  • Mixed-Direction Mission Execution: Structured the course as sequential forward and reverse path segments for T-parking, parallel parking, and final-course navigation.
  • Vehicle-Aware Speed Control: Added endpoint deceleration, direction-specific speed handling, and pitch-based compensation for the slope mission.
  • Field Tuning: Adjusted lateral recovery gain and PID behavior to reduce corner drift and straight-line oscillation on the physical platform.

My Role & Contributions

Rather than treating each maneuver as an isolated script, I helped organize the system around explicit path states, completion conditions, and direction-aware control parameters.

Localization & State Estimation

  • GPS-IMU Sensor Fusion: Connected the heading estimate generated by statusInfo.py, which fused GPS and IMU measurements with a Kalman filter.
  • Unified Heading Topic: Used the filtered /imu_gps_heading output as the vehicle orientation input for waypoint tracking and mission-state decisions.

Mission Sequencing & Path Tracking

  • Segmented Course Execution: Organized the competition course into forward and reverse path files, including separate segments for T-parking and parallel-parking maneuvers.
  • Explicit Path Transitions: Refined completion conditions, stop intervals, path-index resets, and direction changes so the vehicle could move from one maneuver to the next without continuing straight or switching early.
  • Tracking Recovery: Tuned the lateral recovery gain used when the vehicle drifted away from the reference path, while balancing it against overshoot near corners.

Vehicle Control & Field Testing

  • Direction-Aware Speed Profiles: Separated forward and reverse speed behavior so endpoint deceleration did not unintentionally stop an already slow reversing segment.
  • Oscillation Reduction: Iteratively tuned the derivative term and steering response to reduce left-right oscillation on straight sections.
  • Slope Compensation: Used the IMU pitch signal to detect the ramp and apply bounded throttle compensation during climbing and the required timed stop.
  • Traffic-Light-Based Control: Developed and tested logic that stopped the vehicle in response to a detected traffic signal and resumed driving when permitted.

Challenges & Solutions

1 Preventing Premature Transitions Between Reverse and Forward Paths
Challenge

During a reversing segment, the path-completion check could become true at the second-to-last waypoint. If the vehicle was within a broader 0.3 m range of the final point, the system called the final-stop routine before reaching the intended 0.1 m tolerance. The mission then loaded the next forward path while the reverse maneuver was still incomplete.

Solution

The transition logic was tightened so path completion required the actual terminal waypoint and the final distance tolerance, not only a near-terminal index. I separated the stop-and-switch sequence from normal tracking, reset the target index when loading the next segment, and kept forward and reverse completion conditions explicit. This prevented an early state change from turning an unfinished reverse maneuver into a forward command.

2 Holding Position During the Timed Slope Stop
Challenge

One mission required the vehicle to stop on a ramp for a fixed period. A neutral throttle command allowed the vehicle to roll backward under gravity, while a large counter-throttle command made it creep forward. A single fixed braking value therefore could not hold the vehicle reliably on the physical slope.

Solution

I used the IMU pitch value to trigger a dedicated slope-hold state instead of treating the ramp like a normal waypoint stop. During the timed hold, the controller applied a small, bounded counter-throttle near the neutral point, with the compensation tuned to the measured incline. Keeping the command inside a narrow range prevented rollback without applying enough force to move the vehicle forward. The same pitch signal was also used to add controlled speed compensation while climbing.

Reflection

This project showed me that autonomous driving reliability often depends on the boundaries between algorithms. A heading estimate, waypoint follower, parking path, and throttle controller can each work independently, yet the vehicle can still fail if a path finishes too early, a direction flag is not reset, or a braking rule is reused in the wrong state.

The most important lesson was to design mission transitions around both software state and physical vehicle behavior. If I rebuilt the system, I would separate mission sequencing, path tracking, and longitudinal control into clearer modules, add recorded-data regression tests for every transition, and use measured wheel speed or position drift to close the slope-hold loop instead of relying mainly on tuned throttle compensation.

The entire team repeatedly worked through the night at school, so it was especially disappointing that we were unable to complete the parking missions. Heavy rain on competition day also introduced substantial noise into the vehicles' sensor readings, and only a few teams were able to drive reliably. Under those conditions, receiving the Encouragement Award was still meaningful. More importantly, the experience made me curious about how autonomous systems can remain dependable in even harsher weather. Although we did not complete every mission, the strong collaboration within the team made the project a valuable experience.
