Overview

The V2X-Based Cooperative Driving System was developed for the KAIST Mobility Challenge Competition, where our team was honored with the Excellence Award. The core objective of this project was to navigate complex, multi-agent traffic scenarios, such as CAV roundabout entry/exit around a continuously circulating HV and four-way intersection negotiation among CAV1-CAV4, by exclusively leveraging V2X communication and cooperative driving protocols.

As a core software engineer on the team, I spearheaded the development of the decision-making and control pipeline. My primary contributions included designing rule-based collision avoidance logic for intersections, implementing an MPC-inspired Pure Pursuit path tracking algorithm, and refactoring the system architecture to enable dynamic vehicle allocation and rapid parameter tuning in the field.

My Role & Contributions

In this project, I led the implementation of the cooperative decision-making and control pipeline for a multi-CAV driving scenario. My work focused on translating V2X-shared vehicle states into safe, executable behaviors, while also improving the system architecture so the team could deploy, tune, and recover the fleet quickly during field tests.

V2X Decision-Making Logic

  • Roundabout & Intersection Collision Avoidance: Designed rule-based decision logic for allowing CAVs to enter and exit the roundabout without colliding with the circulating HV, while also preventing conflicts among CAV1-CAV4 at four-way intersections.
  • Stop-or-Go Priority Rule: Implemented logic that decides whether the ego CAV should stop and yield or pass first based on relative position, velocity, and proximity to nearby vehicles.
  • V2X State Interpretation: Built decision logic around shared vehicle state information rather than onboard perception alone, allowing the system to respond to surrounding CAV behavior through cooperative communication.

Path Tracking & Vehicle Control

  • Pure Pursuit-Based Predictive Tracking: Implemented an MPC-inspired path-tracking pipeline based on Pure Pursuit, used to follow reference trajectories while handling vehicle-specific tuning constraints.
  • Rule-Based Velocity Publishing: Published velocity commands according to the stop-or-go decision rule when another vehicle entered the front/rear safety range of about 0.5 m.
  • Sim-to-Real Braking Compensation: Developed a dual-parameter sigmoid deceleration strategy to reduce inertial drift that appeared on physical vehicles but not in simulation.
  • Vehicle-Specific Parameter Tuning: Built a tuning architecture that allowed control parameters to be adjusted per vehicle, improving consistency across CAVs with different mechanical responses.

System Architecture & Field Deployment

  • Dynamic Domain ID Allocation: Refactored hardcoded vehicle assignment into an external YAML-based configuration flow, reducing field setup time and avoiding repeated rebuilds.
  • Launch & Recovery Runbook: Standardized the ROS2 launch sequence, CAV bridge startup, race-start service call, and cleanup procedures used during final competition execution.
  • Competition-Day Stabilization: Focused on reproducibility under time pressure by documenting readiness checks, stale process cleanup, DDS shared-memory recovery, and remote host operation.

Challenges & Solutions

1 Overcoming Sim-to-Real Gap in Braking Dynamics
Challenge

In the simulator, a velocity command of zero resulted in an immediate stop. However, in the real-world physical environment, inertial drift occurred due to momentum and friction even after the stop command was issued. This was a critical issue that could lead to collisions during intersection navigation.

Solution

Designed a Dual-Parameter Sigmoid Deceleration algorithm that dynamically adjusts the slope and transition time of the deceleration curve based on the situational context.

  • Dynamically classified Soft Stop and Hard Stop states based on the target velocity condition.
  • Soft Stop: Applied a gentle slope and sufficient transition time to ensure passenger comfort.
  • Hard Stop (Emergency): Applied a steep slope and removed the time delay multiplier to achieve an immediate, hard braking effect, successfully preventing vehicle drift.
2 Dynamic Domain ID Allocation for Field Testing
Challenge

Unlike the simulation, the available physical vehicles (Domain IDs) randomly changed for each field testing session. The initial system relied on hardcoded IDs, requiring tedious code modifications and recompilations every time to assign roles to the 4 vehicles, causing severe inefficiencies.

Solution

Refactored the system by decoupling the ROS2 Domain ID mapping logic from the core codebase, introducing an external YAML config-based initialization architecture. By automatically allocating CAV 1-4 roles based on the config file order, I reduced the setup time to under a minute through simple variable tweaks, eliminating the need for rebuilding.

3 Vehicle-Specific Hyperparameter Architecture
Challenge

Due to mechanical discrepancies in motor performance and sensor noise among the physical vehicles, applying identical path-tracking parameters resulted in inconsistent behaviors, such as lane departure and severe understeering for certain vehicles.

Solution

Engineered a vehicle-specific hyperparameter management architecture. This centralized framework allowed the control system to fine-tune tracking parameters in real-time via sliders for each unique vehicle ID, ensuring uniform and stable platoon driving performance across the entire fleet.

Reflection & Future Work

This project was an invaluable experience that allowed me to confront the physical limitations and complexities of real-world system integration firsthand. By analyzing the system's current limitations, I have established a clear direction for my future growth as a robotics software engineer.

  • Refining the Intersection Priority Logic: Due to time constraints and safety priorities, I adopted a conservative rule-based stop/pass-first policy with fixed safety margins. Moving forward, I aim to implement a more adaptive priority-planning algorithm that considers relative velocity, conflict-zone occupancy, and predicted arrival timing.
  • Expanding Sensor Fusion Capabilities: During the competition, we were restricted to equipping custom sensors on only one ego-vehicle. Since we could not receive rich, synchronized sensor topics from the other shared vehicles, we had to rely heavily on OptiTrack for basic localization. Moving forward, my goal is to develop advanced decision-making algorithms that process a highly diverse array of environmental data through Multi-Sensor Fusion (LiDAR and Vision), rather than being limited to simple positional information.
  • Advancing Control Theory: I recognized the limitations of implementing an MPC-inspired controller based on Pure Pursuit rather than a complete dynamic vehicle model, which deepened my appreciation for advanced control engineering. I plan to study and design Non-Linear MPC (NMPC) systems that can handle complex non-linear vehicle dynamics more rigorously.
  • ROS2 QoS & System Optimization: Deploying the ROS2 network in the field taught me that QoS (Quality of Service) profiles, topic publishing frequencies (Hz), and the computational load of control nodes have a critical impact on the control loop. I intend to dive deeper into System-level Tuning to minimize inter-node communication bottlenecks and optimize execution speeds for highly stable architectures.
