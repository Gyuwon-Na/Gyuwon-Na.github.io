Overview

This project is a camera-driven autonomous racing system developed for the 2026 SEA:ME Hackathon. A compact D-Racer vehicle had to complete a mission track containing a traffic-light start, high-curvature S-bends, a direction-sign-controlled Y-junction, a roundabout, a dynamic obstacle, and a traffic-light finish. Missing a sign, stopping before the obstacle appeared, leaving before it cleared, or failing to complete the roundabout was treated as a mission failure, so the system needed both fast perception and deterministic mission logic.

As the team lead and a core autonomy software engineer, I led the integration of perception, mission-state management, vehicle control, visualization, and hardware interfaces into a unified ROS2 system. The final vehicle recorded a 56-second run, earning the Silver Award and 4th place overall, only 0.03 seconds behind 3rd place.

Key Achievements

  • Silver Award · 4th Overall: Completed the course in 56 seconds, finishing just 0.03 seconds behind the 3rd-place team.
  • 3.3× Faster Perception: Increased the end-to-end perception pipeline from approximately 6 FPS to a stable 20 FPS by identifying bottlenecks across image transport, model inference, ROS2 execution, and visualization.
  • Integrated camera acquisition, classical lane perception, NCNN object detection, temporal filtering, mission FSMs, steering and throttle control, and actuator output.
  • Implemented lane following, traffic-light recognition, direction-sign decisions, Y-junction navigation, dynamic-obstacle handling, and finish-state stopping using a single front camera.
  • Added live ROS2 parameter tuning, visual overlays, detector status topics, and separate debug streams so failures could be diagnosed and corrected during track tests.

My Role & Contributions

My contributions covered the full loop from camera frames to physical steering and throttle commands, with particular emphasis on real-time performance, mission reliability, and rapid field iteration. I also assembled the vehicle hardware and configured the onboard computing platform when the kit was first delivered.

Perception & Scene Understanding

  • Lane Geometry: Built a LAB-based lane pipeline with morphology, Canny edges, Hough features, quadratic curve fitting, road-mask centroids, and historical lane-width tracking.
  • Mission Objects: Integrated NCNN inference for traffic lights and left/right signs, row-based traffic-light color verification, and ArUco marker detection for the dynamic obstacle.
  • Temporal Robustness: Added k-of-n voting, consecutive-frame checks, stale-result rejection, and latched decisions to prevent one noisy frame from changing vehicle behavior.

Mission Planning & Vehicle Control

  • Mission-State Machines: Structured the course as explicit states for waiting, launch, S-curve driving, sign approach, fork commit, obstacle stop, resume, and final stop.
  • Path Tracking: Implemented a Pure Pursuit controller with adaptive lookahead, curvature feedforward, command clamping, and rate limiting for the short-wheelbase platform.
  • Speed Scheduling: Reduced throttle according to steering demand, curvature, and mission phase while keeping acceleration ramped and emergency stops immediate.

Real-Time Systems & Optimization

  • Embedded Inference: Converted and deployed the detector through NCNN, tuned inference resolution and thread counts, and used mission-dependent ROIs to avoid processing irrelevant pixels.
  • Process Isolation: Moved model inference into a spawned process using a fixed shared-memory latest-frame buffer, preventing NCNN initialization and inference from blocking ROS2 callbacks. I then profiled CPU utilization and applied additional system-level optimizations.
  • Fresh-Data ROS2 Design: Used shallow BEST_EFFORT image queues, separated callback groups, capped debug publication, and latest-result queues to prioritize current frames over stale backlog.

Challenges & Solutions

1 Reaching Real-Time Perception on an Embedded Platform
Challenge

Because the vehicle had to run without the SSH dongle, all computation needed to execute on the onboard embedded platform. The first integrated pipeline processed only about 6 FPS. Camera decoding, full-frame model inference, visualization, and ROS2 callbacks competed for the same CPU resources, producing delayed observations and unstable steering. Under heavy load, the board also experienced shutdowns during operation.

Solution

I profiled the pipeline and optimized it as a system rather than only shrinking the model. I introduced NCNN inference, phase-specific ROIs, controlled thread counts, a spawned inference process, a fixed shared-memory latest-frame slot, shallow BEST_EFFORT queues, and rate-limited debug output. Together, these changes raised throughput to a stable 20 FPS, a 3.3× improvement, while keeping control callbacks responsive.

2 Preserving Lane Identity Through Sharp Curves
Challenge

In tight bends, only one boundary could remain visible and could even cross the image center. A naive left-half/right-half classification then mislabeled the right boundary as the left lane, causing the controller to steer farther into the curve and leave the track.

Solution

I combined quadratic x(y) curve fitting with temporal boundary tracking. Previous left and right targets, tracked lane width, the prior lane center, and a curvature hint were used to preserve lane identity when only one curve was visible. Adaptive lookahead and curvature-aware throttle then reduced overshoot on the most aggressive S-bends.

3 Making the Y-Junction Decision Repeatable
Challenge

The direction sign was small at distance, and entering the fork while still following the ordinary lane center biased the vehicle toward the geometrically closer branch. A single incorrect sign frame or a late steering command could therefore select the wrong route and immediately fail the mission.

Solution

I separated sign acquisition from branch execution. The vehicle slowed, stopped after a controlled delay, cleared old detections, and performed a fresh k-of-n stationary vote. Once selected, the LEFT or RIGHT decision was latched and converted into a virtual lane target with a forced directional error. The system maintained that target through a timed commit phase, then reset fork history after a single lane was reacquired.

4 Closing the Training-to-Deployment Gap in Traffic-Light Detection
Challenge

During the first official run, the vehicle failed to launch even after the traffic light turned green. The detector had been trained primarily on close-range traffic-light images, so it did not generalize reliably to the approximately two-meter viewing distance at the starting line. We had also planned a timeout-based fallback launch, but a communication gap within the team meant that the fallback was not integrated before the run.

Solution

Rather than retraining the NCNN model under competition time constraints, I introduced a dedicated traffic-light ROI. The system cropped the expected light region and rescaled it to the detector input size, increasing the apparent object scale and enabling reliable inference from farther away.

Reflection

The event also used an explicitly AI-assisted development workflow. I used coding agents to accelerate implementation and refactoring, but physical testing remained the source of truth. This reinforced an important engineering habit: generated code must still be reviewed against architecture, timing measurements, telemetry, and real vehicle behavior rather than accepted simply because it compiles. Claude Code was also useful in areas that were initially unfamiliar to me, including CPU utilization analysis, battery-voltage monitoring, and power-related debugging. With its assistance, I traced repeated connection losses to the vehicle's power and resource constraints and resolved the underlying issue.

The hackathon also showed me that team performance depends on managing energy and working rhythms, not only technical ability. Our team avoided major conflict, but sustained sleep deprivation still made communication more fragile and occasionally brought us close to unnecessary tension. Observing similar scheduling conflicts in other teams reinforced the importance of setting realistic work rotations, protecting rest, and addressing friction early during high-pressure development.

  • Quantitative Validation: I would add automated rosbag replay benchmarks for lane error, mission-event precision, control latency, and end-to-end success rate so parameter changes can be compared with more than lap time alone.
  • More Predictive Control: The current controller is intentionally lightweight and competition-oriented. A future version could use a calibrated bicycle model with MPC to optimize steering and speed jointly under actuator and track-boundary constraints.
  • Stronger Localization & Fusion: Adding wheel odometry and IMU estimates would make roundabout progress, fork commitment, and temporary lane-loss recovery less dependent on visual heuristics.
  • Production-Oriented ROS2 Execution: I would move more hot-path components to C++, evaluate composable nodes and zero-copy transport, and define explicit QoS contracts and watchdogs for each data stream.
