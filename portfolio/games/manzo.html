Game Development

MANZO

Jun 2024 - Jun 2025 Technical Lead GAM200 - 250 Project
C++ OpenGL Custom Engine RenderDoc

Overview

MANZO is a rhythm-based Metroidvania psychological horror game where the player hunts fish with dash movement, avoids underwater threats, and uncovers the secrets of the sea. Resources earned through hunting can be sold to merchants, then used to buy modules and skills that gradually make deeper exploration possible.

Key Achievements

  • State-Driven Gameplay: Structured gameplay logic around explicit states and tunable parameters.
  • Player-Facing Integration: Integrated player-facing systems with rendering, input, and scene flow.
  • Rendering Diagnostics: Used debugging and rendering tools to investigate visual and runtime issues.

My Role

I worked as the Technical Lead, contributing across rendering systems and core gameplay algorithms. My work focused on building reusable engine features while supporting the game's rhythm movement, exploration loop, fish behavior, and module-driven progression.

  • Developed engine support for quest windows and icons so UI content could be added and managed more easily.
  • Implemented shader-based visibility limits that intensify as the player moves deeper underwater.
  • Laid the foundation for the OpenGL rendering engine and debugged the rendering pipeline with RenderDoc.
  • Designed fish movement patterns and module-related gameplay patterns for the exploration loop.

Technical Highlights

Vector Font Rendering

Implemented custom vector-font rendering to give the game smoother and more scalable text than bitmap-font rendering could provide.

3D Audio Cues

Built positional audio feedback for Morse-code search sequences, increasing volume near spawn points and using stereo direction to guide the player left or right.

Navigation AI

Implemented AI movement for fish and other non-player objects so they could generate paths, avoid terrain collision, and support flocking-style motion.

Challenges & Solutions

1 Scope and Planning
Challenge

Early in planning, everyone had ambitious ideas and the design grew larger than the schedule could support. As deadlines approached, the team needed a clearer way to decide what the game should actually deliver.

Solution

We reduced the game's scope and spent time redesigning around the experience we wanted most. Weekly meetings and Notion task tracking helped us clarify ownership, report progress, and divide work by schedule.

2 OpenGL Rendering
Challenge

While implementing the OpenGL rendering engine, object transform matrices and rendering order became tangled, causing visible stutter and unstable frame behavior.

Solution

We rebuilt parts of the rendering logic with guidance from our professor and used RenderDoc to inspect the pipeline directly. That process helped us identify where the frame data was going wrong instead of guessing from code alone.

3 In-Game AI
Challenge

Fish needed to avoid terrain on their own, but the avoidance algorithms I first tested did not produce the behavior I wanted. At first, it was also difficult to understand exactly where the AI logic was failing.

Solution

I represented the map as walkable and non-walkable regions, then constrained fish movement to valid areas. To avoid checking the entire map every frame, I used a quadtree structure and applied the same idea to the flocking system. This interest later expanded into my autonomous-driving club work.

Reflection

MANZO also taught me the importance of defining a clear product vision from the beginning. Because everyone had ambitious ideas, the initial scope expanded rapidly and the project gradually became a collection of unrelated features rather than a cohesive game experience. I realized that successful software is not built by adding every possible feature, but by maintaining a consistent direction and introducing differentiation within a unified vision.

This experience also showed me how difficult scope and schedule management become in long-term projects. As the project grew, coordinating priorities, estimating development effort, and maintaining progress became increasingly challenging. I learned that controlling the scope early and making deliberate trade-offs are just as important as implementing new features.
