Improving Lane Detection with Morphological Operations and Adaptive Thresholding

A lightweight preprocessing study that combines morphological closing with frame-adaptive Otsu binarization to recover broken lane markings, suppress illumination-driven noise, and stabilize a classical Hough-based lane-detection pipeline.

01

Overview

A small change in a fragile classical pipeline

Classical lane detection can be fast and interpretable, but its performance often collapses when a fixed color range meets shadows, tunnels, overexposure, faded paint, or broken lane segments.

This study focused on the preprocessing stage rather than replacing the entire lane detector. The core idea was that two inexpensive operations could address complementary failure modes: morphological closing could reconnect fragmented lane pixels, while Otsu binarization could select a threshold independently for each frame.

The resulting binary mask was passed directly to a Hough-based line detector. By omitting the Canny stage, the pipeline also tested whether a cleaner adaptive mask could preserve useful lane structure without adding unnecessary edge-processing overhead.

Problem
Fixed HSV thresholds break under rapid illumination changes and fragmented markings.
Core idea
Reconnect lane regions first, then calculate a frame-specific binary threshold.
Evaluation
TuSimple scenes with low light, overexposure, and partial structural shadows.
Output
Cleaner lane candidates, Hough lines, and a more stable steering estimate.
02

Problem Recognition

Two failure modes behind fragile lane masks

01

Color thresholds trade recall for noise

Narrow HSV ranges suppress background noise, but they also remove valid lane pixels when paint is faded or lighting shifts. Broader ranges recover more lane pixels, yet introduce road texture, reflections, vehicles, and environmental clutter into the mask.

02

Lighting changes faster

The second failure is temporal: a threshold that works in open sunlight can fail a moment later under a bridge. The detector therefore needs both structural continuity and frame-level adaptation, not only a single carefully tuned color interval.

03

Pipeline

Closing before Otsu, then direct geometric detection

1HSV Candidate Mask

Extract likely lane pixels using color-space constraints.

25×5 Closing

Reconnect narrow gaps and strengthen lane continuity.

3Otsu Binarization

Choose a frame-specific threshold from the image histogram.

4Hough Transform

Detect left and right lane-line candidates from the binary mask.

5Steering Estimate

Compare the detected lane center with the image center.

1

Continuity

Morphological Closing

Closing applies dilation followed by erosion. It fills small holes and joins nearby fragments while retaining the overall shape of the candidate lane region.

2

Adaptation

Otsu Thresholding

Otsu selects the threshold that best separates two intensity classes. Recomputing it per frame allows the mask to react to abrupt brightness changes.

04

Experimental Questions

Benchmarking operator order, noise, and lane-center stability

The study used TuSimple road images and compared four preprocessing variants. The goal was not only to test whether each operation helped, but also whether Closing → Otsu behaved differently from Otsu → Closing.

Variants

Four pipelines

  • Conventional fixed-threshold HSV method
  • Closing only
  • Closing followed by Otsu
  • Otsu followed by closing
Scenarios

Illumination stress cases

  • Low light under a structure
  • Strong overexposure after leaving shade
  • Partial lane occlusion by structural shadow
Measures

Accuracy and mask quality

  • Mean centerline pixel-distance error
  • Noise-pixel count and noise penalty
  • Average processing time per frame
05

Observed Results

Where the Mask Started Working

Low-light environment

Figure 1

The fixed-threshold and closing-only pipelines failed to separate the lane pixels in the dark region. Pipelines containing adaptive thresholding retained meaningful lane candidates. Closing → Otsu reached a mean centerline-distance error of 4.84 px, matching the alternative Otsu-based order while processing each frame approximately 1 ms faster in this comparison.

Overexposed environment

Figure 2

Most variants produced a visible lane estimate, but the proposed order generated the fewest non-lane pixels. It achieved a mean centerline-distance error of 13.18 px, slightly below the 13.2 px result of the other Otsu-based ordering, while also recording lower average frame time.

Partial structural shadow

Figure 3

Visually, the methods produced similar lane lines, but their false-positive distribution differed sharply. The conventional pipeline recorded a 73.7 px noise penalty, whereas the Otsu-based pipelines reduced it to 9.1 px. Total noise pixels also decreased by approximately 30%, indicating a much cleaner binary representation.

Main finding: the benefit came from pairing complementary operations. Closing repaired the spatial structure of the lane mask, while Otsu adapted the decision boundary to the current illumination.

Future Work

What the preprocessing step still cannot solve

1Dependence on visible lane color
Limitation

HSV-based candidates remain unreliable when lane paint is severely faded, damaged, or covered by dust and leaves.

Direction

Combine color evidence with learned structure cues or complementary sensors rather than relying on the mask alone.

2Limited real-road validation
Limitation

The evaluation focused on selected TuSimple conditions and does not establish robustness across weather, road types, or camera systems.

Direction

Validate on continuous driving sequences and measure temporal stability, runtime, and steering behavior on physical vehicles.

08

Reflection

A lesson in improving systems without overcomplicating them

What This Study Revealed
  • Two inexpensive operations can address different failure modes more effectively than adding a large new model.
  • The order of preprocessing steps changes the statistical input seen by every later stage.
Future Directions
  • Use temporal consistency so threshold and lane estimates do not fluctuate independently frame by frame.
  • Evaluate on physical autonomous-driving hardware with end-to-end control latency.
  • Fuse camera cues with LiDAR or learned lane representations in visually degraded scenes.
